# Skill: deliverables (memo, storyline, slides)

## Answer first (pyramid)
Lead with the answer to the client's question, then 3–5 supporting arguments, each backed
by evidence below it. A reader who stops after the first paragraph should know what to do
and why.

## Always commit to a best answer
The client is paying for a recommendation, so give one even when the evidence is thin, and
let the confidence level carry the uncertainty. The opening block is, in order:
1. **Recommendation:** what the client should do, in 2–4 sentences.
2. **Confidence (high / medium / low) and why:** how strong the evidence is and what is
   missing. Thin evidence lowers the confidence; it does not remove the recommendation.
3. **What it rests on:** the 2–3 pieces of evidence (fact, analysis or assumption ids)
   that carry the answer.
4. **What would change it:** the findings that would flip or soften the recommendation.
5. **Next steps:** including the data still to collect, and what each piece would settle.

"Collect more data before deciding" is never the recommendation. It is a next step that
follows a committed recommendation ("Do X; confirm with Y before full roll-out").
Committing means reasoning from the evidence you have: the number rules below still apply
in full, so a recommendation with a missing figure says so in words instead of inventing it.

## Action titles
Every slide or section title states the insight, not the topic: "Dealer incentives, not
price, explain the share loss" rather than "Market share analysis". A good title names a
cause or an implication. Reading only the titles, in order, must tell the whole story.

## One message per slide
Each slide makes one point, shows the data that proves it and highlights that data point.
Cite the source on the slide. No text walls; put the most important element top-left.

## Storyline arc
Context → the client's situation and complication → root causes / key findings →
options considered (and why the others were eliminated: financial, operational,
technical, ethical feasibility, time) → recommendation → implementation plan (timeline,
owners, dependencies) → risks and mitigations → expected impact. Draft the titles first;
if two consecutive titles don't connect, the logic has a gap.

## Recommendations
Bucket ideas into one coherent strategy instead of listing everything. Separate short-term
from long-term actions. Quantify the impact (NPV, payback, margin points) with sandbox
numbers.

## Honesty rules (stricter than competition decks)
- Every number traces to a fact id, an analysis output or a labelled assumption.
- Disclose material risks even when there is no mitigation; mark them as open.
- State confidence and what new data would change the answer.

## Calibrated confidence, clean text
- Confidence is about whether the evidence settles the decision, not about how much more
  could be learned. Low means a named missing fact would probably flip the answer; a gap
  that would not change the recommendation belongs in the next steps, not in caveats.
- Show numbers the way a reader takes them in: two or three significant figures, the exact
  value staying in the model.
- Evidence ids are the audit trail: they sit in the references, never in the sentences the
  client reads.
- One list of open questions, each asked once.
